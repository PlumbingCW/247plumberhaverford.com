What a sewer camera inspection costs and why it varies

A sewer camera inspection costs less than most repairs and more than a quick look at a cleanout. The price depends on access, length, whether the line needs cleaning first and what report you receive. We do not publish a fixed number because no honest plumber can price your lateral without seeing the access.

This guide lists the factors that move the price and what you should expect for the money, so you can compare quotes from any plumber in Haverford, PA.

What moves the price

Four things decide the final number.

Access

A clear outdoor cleanout is quick. Pulling a toilet to get in adds time.

Length and layout

A long lateral with turns takes longer to run and record.

Cleaning first

A line coated with grease or full of roots may need a jet pass before the camera can see.

Report

A recorded video and a marked locate cost more than a live look with no record.

What a good inspection includes

A good inspection runs the camera as far as it can go, records the footage and tells you how far it traveled and why it stopped. A locator on the camera head lets the plumber mark depth and position on the ground above.

You should leave with an explanation of what you saw: roots, cracks, offsets, low spots or a clean pipe. Ask for a copy of the footage, which you can show an insurer, a second plumber or a home inspector.

Why a camera look matters under Haverford Township rules

Haverford Township Code treats the sewer service lateral as the property of the customer. The Township also says it is generally not liable for sewer, drain or sump pump damage and notes that standard homeowners policies do not cover sewer backups or flooding without an endorsement.

That puts the cost of finding a problem on the owner, and it makes a camera look before a purchase or after a backup a sensible expense. Keep the footage, since it documents the condition of the pipe at a point in time.

When an inspection is worth paying for

The best times to schedule are before buying a house, after a second clog in a year, when a sewer smell or soft ground appears outside and when a repair quote seems high. In each case, footage turns a guess into evidence.

If sewage is backing up now, the plumber clears the line first and runs the camera as part of the visit. See our sewer camera inspection page for what the visit looks like and sewer line cleaning for emergency backups.

What the camera cannot do

A camera sees the inside of the pipe. It cannot tell you the soil outside, and it cannot go past a collapse. Some cleanouts are too small for the rod, and some lines have bends the camera cannot turn.

A good report says where the camera stopped and why. If it stopped early, ask about a second access point. If the footage shows damage, the next step is a repair plan, which is explained on sewer line repair.

Questions that reveal how a plumber really prices

Ask whether the quote includes cleaning if the line is too dirty to see. Ask whether the footage is recorded and delivered and whether the locate marks depth and position. Ask what happens if the camera cannot pass a blockage.

A plumber who answers each of these clearly is pricing the whole job. A plumber who says the price is a flat number for any house is leaving something out, and you will find out when the invoice arrives.

Inspections for home purchases

If you are buying a house in Haverford Township or a nearby town, a camera inspection of the lateral gives you leverage and information. A clean line means one less worry. A damaged line lets you negotiate a repair credit before closing.

Share the footage with your agent and your home inspector. Ask the seller whether the lateral has ever been cleaned, relined or repaired, and whether any records exist.

What happens after you see the footage

The plumber ranks what you saw. Minor roots or scale may only need a cleaning. A belly that holds water may need monitoring or a repair. A crack or offset needs a plan and a quote.

Do not feel pressed to decide on the spot. Take the footage home, compare it with a second opinion if the repair is large and ask for the quote in writing with the method and the warranty spelled out.

Industry standards for sewer inspection reports

Many inspectors code their findings using the NASSCO Pipeline Assessment Certification Program, known as PACP, a common standard for describing pipe defects. Ask whether the plumber uses a recognized coding system for the report.

A coded report makes it easier for a second plumber or an engineer to read the findings and compare quotes.

Tips for getting the most from the visit

Know where your cleanout is and clear the path to it. Tell the plumber about past clogs, nearby trees and the age of the house. Stay for the review, because the footage makes more sense with a plumber pointing at it.
