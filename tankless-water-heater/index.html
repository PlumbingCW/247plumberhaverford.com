Tankless water heaters in Haverford, PA

A tankless water heater heats water as you use it instead of keeping a tank hot all day. The unit is wall-mounted and small, and it can supply hot water for as long as the flow is running. In Haverford, PA the install is a good fit for homeowners who want space back or a household that runs out of hot water.

It is not the right choice for every house. This page explains what tankless does well, what it costs to set up and what to ask before you commit.

How tankless differs from a tank

A tank stores heated water, so the first shower is hot and the fourth may not be. A tankless unit fires a burner or heating element only when you open a tap, so the temperature stays steady as long as the flow is within its range.

The trade-off is flow. A tankless unit can only heat so many gallons a minute, so two showers and a washer can exceed a small unit. A plumber sizes the heater to your peak demand, not your average day.

What the install involves

Gas tankless units often need a larger gas line than a tank because they burn more fuel at once. They also need a sealed vent that is often stainless or plastic, depending on the model. Electric units need a heavy circuit and a panel with room to spare.

Because of this, a tankless install is more than a swap. The plumber checks your gas meter, line size, vent path and electrical capacity, then gives you a written scope. See water heater installation for how the plan comes together.

Hard water and descaling

Tankless heaters have narrow heat exchangers that scale in hard water. Scale cuts flow and efficiency, and in time it can damage the unit. Many manufacturers call for periodic descaling, and your warranty may depend on it.

Ask your plumber to test your water and tell you what interval to expect. We do not quote a hardness figure from memory, because it varies by street and by the water source feeding your house. A simple test gives you a real number.

Code points a tankless install must meet

A tankless unit still needs a relief valve, safe venting and a code-compliant gas or electric connection. Pennsylvania's Uniform Construction Code relies on the International Code Council's model codes, including the International Plumbing Code, or IPC.

Manufacturer instructions carry the force of code for venting and clearances, so a good installer follows them to the letter. Ask to see the sheet before the install.

When a tankless heater is not the best fit

A heater that serves a house with very low flow fixtures may not fire reliably, because many units need a minimum flow to start. A house with a long run between the heater and the far bathroom will still wait for hot water to arrive, so a recirculation line might be worth discussing.

If your goal is lower cost and your tank is sound, a tank replacement may be more sensible. See water heater replacement for that route, and water heater repair if your current tank just needs a part.
